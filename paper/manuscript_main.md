# Abstract

Partitioned-heritability studies have shown which tissues carry the heritability of complex traits, but whether the regulatory layer whose annotations are most informative within a tissue differs between traits has not been tested, because measured annotations for different layers come from different biosamples. Sequence-to-function models predict the allelic effect of every variant on several regulatory layers with a single model, so that layers can be compared on the same variants within the same tissues. We grouped AlphaGenome Atlas predictions into 12 lineages and six regulatory layers and partitioned the heritability of 11 complex traits with stratified LD score regression. Traits localized largely to the expected lineages, and within these lineages the relative informativeness of predicted transcription-initiation (CAGE) and chromatin-accessibility (DNase) effects differed between traits (*P* = 3.6 × 10^−8^): DNase effects were the more informative for height, and CAGE effects, to varying degrees, for the other traits. The differences held with cell-type-matched layers, with continuous annotations, at distal SNPs, without the most influential regions, conditional on predicted activity and with larger GWAS for six traits. For three related traits whose layers had been predicted in advance, the predicted contrast held (*P* = 1.6 × 10^−7^). Predicted annotations carried information beyond measured annotations and fine-mapped tissue and cell-type eQTLs. Most of this information lay where activity was predicted; within predicted active elements, larger predicted effects carried additional heritability for height (accessibility) and two psychiatric traits (transcription initiation). Aggregating sequence-model predictions genome-wide can therefore place traits on a regulatory-layer axis as well as a tissue axis.

# Introduction

Most heritability of complex traits lies in non-coding variation that is thought to act through gene regulation [@maurano2012; @finucane2015]. Stratified LD score regression (S-LDSC) partitions SNP heritability across functional annotations using GWAS summary statistics [@finucane2015; @gazal2017]. Applied to tissue-specific chromatin and expression data, it and earlier enrichment methods have mapped many traits to the tissues and cell types in which their associated variants and heritability are enriched [@trynka2013; @roadmap2015; @finucane2018]. These maps show where a trait acts. They say less about the regulatory layer through which it acts within that tissue: whether chromatin accessibility, active histone marks, transcription initiation or steady-state expression carries the heritability.

The difficulty is partly one of measurement. Different layers have largely been profiled by different consortia in different sets of tissues and cell types. Roadmap Epigenomics measured several histone marks in the same epigenomes [@roadmap2015], but transcription initiation was profiled mainly by FANTOM5 in other biosamples [@forrest2014; @andersson2014]. A comparison between layers is therefore often also a comparison between biosample collections. Differences between traits in the most enriched mark or layer have therefore been noted descriptively [@trynka2013; @trynka2015; @finucane2015; @dong2022], but, to our knowledge, whether the relative informativeness of layers for heritability differs between traits has not been tested formally. The question matters because GWAS signals often fail to colocalize with expression QTLs [@umans2021; @connally2022; @mostafavi2023], and the cis-genetic component of assayed expression mediates only about 11% of heritability [@yao2020]. Part of the remaining signal may act through expression in contexts that have not been assayed [@umans2021], and part may act through other regulatory layers.

Sequence-to-function models offer a partial way around the measurement problem. They predict the allelic effect of every variant on many regulatory readouts with a single model and a common scoring rule, so that layers can be compared on the same variants within the same tissues [@avsec2021; @chen2022; @linder2025; @avsec2026]. Because the models are trained on the same consortium data, the biosamples profiled for each layer still overlap only partly; we therefore compared layers within lineages rather than within individual biosamples. Allelic-effect annotations from two earlier models, DeepSEA and Basenji, added little heritability information once baseline annotations, measured chromatin annotations and, where informative, the models' predictions for the reference sequence were included: only predicted H3K4me3 from Basenji remained significant, and transcription initiation, which Basenji also predicts, was left out of the analysis [@dey2020]. Applying the newest models genome-wide has been limited by computation [@ye2026]. The AlphaGenome Atlas now provides precomputed predictions for every possible single-nucleotide variant in the human genome [@cheng2026], which makes it possible to fit predicted annotations of several layers jointly within the same lineage, together with the predicted activity of the same tracks, and to ask whether their relative informativeness for heritability differs between traits. In previous work we used the same predictions to examine Alzheimer's disease (AD) loci one at a time and found that association tracked predicted expression effects, and less robustly splicing effects, but that the cellular context of the fine-mapped variants could not be resolved at the level of single loci [@choi2026]. Aggregation across the genome may provide the statistical power that single loci lack.

Here we built lineage-by-layer annotations from AlphaGenome Atlas predictions, grouping tissues and cell types into 12 lineages, and asked four questions (Fig. 1). Does heritability localize to the expected lineages? Within a lineage, does the relative informativeness of predicted layers differ between traits? If so, does the resulting map predict the layer profile of traits that were not used to build it? And do the predicted allelic effects carry information beyond measured annotations of matched cell types and tissues, beyond fine-mapped eQTLs and beyond the predicted activity of the same tracks?

# Results

## Allele-specific annotations from AlphaGenome Atlas

We retrieved AlphaGenome Atlas predictions for all 5,828,323 SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 5% in the 1000 Genomes European reference panel [@kg2015] for which the reference and alternative alleles could be resolved (97.9% of 5,956,110). We stored per-track allelic effects for 1,748 tracks: DNase-seq (305), CAGE (546), RNA-seq (371) and histone ChIP–seq for H3K27ac, H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 (526). After excluding cell lines (372 tracks) and 64 tracks from mixed or undefined biosamples, we assigned the remaining 1,312 tracks from 433 biosamples to 12 lineages by rules on biosample names and types that were fixed before any heritability analysis (Supplementary Table S2). The brain-tissue lineage, for example, comprised 38 adult and fetal brain regions; cultured neural cells, both in vitro differentiated (neurons and neural progenitors) and primary (astrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursors, Schwann cells and neurons), formed a separate neural-cell lineage. The myeloid lineage comprised monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, granulocytes, mast cells and myeloid progenitors; the Atlas contains no microglia tracks.

For each lineage and layer we took, for each SNP, the maximum absolute allelic effect across the tracks of that lineage and layer and annotated the top 5% of SNPs. We partitioned the heritability of 11 traits (Supplementary Table S1) conditional on baselineLD v2.2 and report τ\*, the change in per-SNP heritability per standard deviation of the annotation, scaled by the mean per-SNP heritability [@gazal2017]. Because the baseline model already contains fine-mapped eQTLs [@hormozdiari2018], FANTOM5 enhancers [@andersson2014], promoters, TSS proximity and conservation, a positive τ\* indicates that an annotation is informative for heritability beyond these annotations. Each analysis was specified in a dated protocol before it was run, but later protocols were written with knowledge of earlier results; Methods and Supplementary Table S6 give the level of evidence of each analysis, and post hoc analyses are labelled as such.

## Traits localize to the expected lineages

We first annotated each lineage with a single score that combined DNase, H3K27ac, H3K4me1 and CAGE predictions (Fig. 2). Of 132 trait–lineage cells, 19 were significant after correction for the 132 tests (false discovery rate < 0.05). Ten of the 11 traits had their strongest enrichment in the lineage expected from their biology. Monocyte count was enriched in the myeloid lineage (τ\* = +2.82, *P* = 1 × 10^−10^), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and rheumatoid arthritis in the lymphoid lineage (+3.43, *P* = 5 × 10^−8^; +2.48, *P* = 3 × 10^−4^), LDL cholesterol in liver (+3.21, *P* = 4 × 10^−5^) and height in adipose, skeletal muscle, stromal (mesenchymal) and cardiovascular tissues (+1.18 to +1.77).

Educational attainment, schizophrenia and neuroticism were enriched in brain tissue (+0.87, *P* = 9 × 10^−5^; +1.55, *P* = 3 × 10^−9^; +1.42, *P* = 7 × 10^−5^), and body mass index (BMI) showed nominal brain-tissue enrichment (+0.53, *P* = 0.037). Bipolar disorder was the exception: in its small GWAS (*N* = 16,731), its strongest enrichment was in the myeloid lineage (+2.22, *P* = 0.001), with brain tissue second (+1.91, *P* = 0.009), whereas in a recent, larger GWAS [@oconnell2025] brain tissue ranked first (+1.76, *P* = 4 × 10^−13^; Supplementary Table S16). In contrast, the lineage of cultured neural cells was not enriched for any brain trait (τ\* between −0.62 and +0.15). Brain-trait heritability was therefore captured by predictions in adult and fetal brain tissue, not by predictions in cultured neural cells. These enrichments remained when we also conditioned on an annotation of SNPs with large predicted effects in any lineage (Supplementary Fig. S1), so they reflect lineage-specific predictions rather than a general tendency of large-effect SNPs to carry heritability.

AD heritability was enriched in the myeloid lineage (+2.98, *P* = 6 × 10^−6^) and, more weakly, in the lymphoid lineage (+1.22, *P* = 0.005), in agreement with earlier evidence that AD risk acts through myeloid cells and microglia [@gjoneska2015; @huang2017; @tansey2018; @novikova2021]. Enrichment in brain tissue was not detected (+0.88, *P* = 0.068). Its point estimate was similar to that of educational attainment (+0.87), but the standard error was larger, partly because AD has a low observed-scale heritability (0.026), so the result does not exclude a brain-tissue contribution. In an initial analysis with monocyte and macrophage tracks, the myeloid enrichment was robust to the annotation threshold, to conditioning on the overall AlphaGenome variant-impact score, to inclusion of the *APOE* region and, in a post hoc analysis, to removal of all SNPs within 1 Mb of genome-wide significant loci (Supplementary Table S12).

## Within a lineage, the relative informativeness of predicted layers differs between traits

We then separated the layers within each lineage. In single-layer models (72 lineage–layer annotations per trait), 54 of 792 trait–cell combinations were significant (Supplementary Fig. S2). The strongest cell was a CAGE cell for 10 of 11 traits: brain-tissue CAGE for every brain trait, myeloid CAGE for AD, monocyte count and IBD, lymphoid CAGE for rheumatoid arthritis and liver CAGE for LDL cholesterol. For height it was stromal DNase. Predicted RNA-seq annotations were rarely informative: one of 132 RNA cells was significant. Because RNA-seq scores are gene-level, whereas CAGE and DNase scores are computed in a 501-bp window around the variant, this difference should not be read as evidence about the importance of steady-state expression.

Single-layer models cannot show which layer is most informative, because annotations of different layers in the same lineage overlap and because layers differ in the number of tracks. CAGE has the most tracks in most lineages, so its maximum-effect score could be favoured for that reason alone. We therefore fitted three distinct layers that were available in every lineage analysed, CAGE (transcription initiation), DNase (chromatin accessibility) and H3K27ac (active promoters and enhancers), jointly in the lineage relevant to each trait. In these joint models, a larger τ\* for one layer means that its annotation carries more heritability information conditional on the other two layers and the baseline; it does not by itself show that the layer is the route through which risk acts. For the same reason, a negative τ\* in a joint model indicates less heritability than expected given the other layers and the baseline, not a protective process. We reduced the track-number imbalance by resampling up to three units per layer (a unit was one track for DNase and H3K27ac and one CAGE library, with both strands, for CAGE; all units were used when fewer were available, as for the two liver CAGE libraries) 20 times, and averaged estimates and jackknife replicates over the 20 resamples. H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 were left out because they were collinear with H3K27ac, and RNA-seq because its gene-level annotations carried almost no signal and are not directly comparable with the window-based scores. These choices, the tests and the classification rule were fixed before the joint models were run, but after the single-layer results and joint six-layer models of the same GWAS had been seen. The layer types of the 11 map traits are therefore an exploratory classification; the prospective test of the map is the prediction for three new traits described below.

The layer profiles differed between traits (multivariate Cochran's *Q* on the CAGE − DNase and CAGE − H3K27ac contrasts = 74.3, 20 degrees of freedom, *P* = 3.6 × 10^−8^; *P* = 7.6 × 10^−6^ with an *F* approximation; Fig. 3). All tests between traits used a joint block jackknife that accounts for covariance between traits from shared samples and shared genetic architecture (Methods and Supplementary Table S10). The CAGE − DNase contrast ranged from −1.43 ± 0.52 for height to +3.99 ± 0.86 for monocyte count (Fig. 3b). Height was the only map trait for which DNase effects were the more informative: stromal DNase τ\* was +1.92 ± 0.37, against +0.49 ± 0.32 for CAGE and +0.04 ± 0.31 for H3K27ac, and DNase ranked first in 19 of 20 resamples. For the other ten traits CAGE effects were the more informative, to degrees that differed between traits: the contrast was between +0.8 and +1.5 for educational attainment, schizophrenia, neuroticism and BMI, around +2 for IBD and rheumatoid arthritis, and between +3 and +4 for LDL cholesterol, AD, bipolar disorder and monocyte count. Layer annotations within a lineage overlapped partly (Jaccard index 0.08–0.38), but after the baseline annotations were regressed out, their LD scores were only moderately correlated (partial correlation 0.03–0.59; variance inflation factor between layers 1.07–1.74; Supplementary Table S13), so the joint models could separate the layers. Of the three pairwise contrasts specified in the protocol, the CAGE − DNase contrast differed between height and four brain traits (educational attainment, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and neuroticism; +2.75 ± 0.58, *P* = 2 × 10^−6^) and between height and AD (+4.51 ± 1.51, *P* = 0.003), whereas the CAGE − H3K27ac contrast did not differ between the two lymphoid and the two myeloid traits (−1.88 ± 0.90, *P* = 0.037; α = 0.017). In the control analyses below, the first of these contrasts held throughout, whereas the second weakened when a few genomic regions were removed and after conditioning on binary predicted activity (Table 1).

As an exploratory summary, the classification rule (whose types label the annotation profile, not the mechanism) called seven traits CAGE type and height DNase type and left three unresolved (neuroticism, IBD and rheumatoid arthritis), all of which had CAGE as the most frequent top layer. The resample counts describe how stable the ranking was to the choice of tracks within a lineage; they are not probabilities that the ranking holds in other cell types. The averages had converged: in every trait, the top layer of the running average did not change after the second resample, and the variation due to resampling was a median of 0.20 times the jackknife standard error. With all tracks instead of resampled tracks, the top layer was the same for all 11 traits, so the map was not driven by track numbers. In the liver lineage, CAGE ranked first for LDL cholesterol although it had fewer units (two libraries) than DNase and H3K27ac (three tracks each). In a post hoc leave-one-trait-out analysis, heterogeneity remained after removing any single trait: *Q* was between 56 and 74 when any trait other than height was removed, and 38.5 without height (18 degrees of freedom, *P* = 0.003; Supplementary Table S5). The heterogeneity therefore has two parts: the position of height on the DNase side, and differences in the degree of CAGE dominance among the other traits.

Because the 5% threshold is arbitrary and the strongest predictions of sequence models are the most reliable [@drusinsky2026], we also fitted the percentiles of the three layers jointly as continuous annotations, which use the rank of every SNP rather than a cut-off. Layer profiles still differed between traits (*Q* = 43.6, 20 degrees of freedom, *P* = 0.002), the CAGE − DNase contrast had the sign of the main map in all 14 traits and in the larger GWAS for five traits, the brain traits still leaned more towards CAGE than height (+1.85 ± 0.60, *P* = 0.002) and the validation contrast held (+2.59 ± 0.59, one-sided *P* = 6 × 10^−6^; Supplementary Table S17 and Supplementary Fig. S4). Results with stricter binary thresholds and with non-overlapping bins of the percentile, among which the CAGE lean of the brain, lipid and lymphoid traits was less consistent for the SNPs with the largest predicted effects, are given in Supplementary Tables S7 and S17 and Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4.

## The layer differences are robust to the choice of cell types, lineages, SNPs, genomic regions and GWAS

We tested the layer differences against six alternative explanations, each with criteria fixed before the analysis (Table 1 and Methods). Because annotations of different layers come from partly different biosamples, we rebuilt the three layers from matched cell types or regions with one unit per layer (Supplementary Table S8); because traits were compared in different lineages, we refitted every trait in every lineage in which it was enriched (Supplementary Table S9); because CAGE predictions may reflect promoter proximity, we split each annotation at 1 kb from annotated TSSs (Supplementary Table S11); because the contrasts might rest on a few regions, we removed the five most influential jackknife blocks (Supplementary Table S14); and because six map traits had older or smaller GWAS, we refitted them with recent, larger European-ancestry GWAS of height, BMI, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, LDL cholesterol and AD, which include the earlier participants and therefore test robustness rather than independent replication (Supplementary Table S16). In every analysis, layer profiles differed between traits, the brain traits leaned more towards CAGE than height, and DNase remained the most informative layer for height (Table 1). Three details qualify this. Within a shared lineage, the top layer itself depended on the lineage (H3K27ac often ranked first for height outside the stromal lineage), so what carries over between lineages is the relative position of traits on the CAGE–DNase axis, not the layer type. The contrast between AD and height weakened when five blocks were removed (*P* = 0.064); the most influential block contained the *MS4A* locus, and without the five blocks myeloid CAGE no longer exceeded DNase, so the excess of CAGE over DNase for AD rests largely on a few regions. With the larger GWAS, the CAGE lean of bipolar disorder was much smaller than in its small GWAS (+0.67 ± 0.33 against +3.79 ± 0.99) and height's DNase lean was less distinct (one-sided *P* = 0.054), whereas AD's position was unchanged.

## The layer map predicts the layer of new traits

To test whether the map predicts, we chose three traits that were not used to build it and fixed predictions in a dated protocol before analysing them. Each new trait was paired with an analogous trait already in the map, and its predicted layer was set to the layer type of that analogue: heel bone mineral density (BMD) [@morris2019] from height (stromal lineage, DNase type), total cholesterol from UK Biobank [@zoodsma2025] from LDL cholesterol (liver, CAGE type), and neutrophil count [@vuckovic2020] from monocyte count (myeloid, CAGE type). The BMD and total cholesterol GWAS share no samples with their analogues, whose GWAS preceded UK Biobank [@langoallen2010; @teslovich2010]. Neutrophil count and monocyte count come from the same study and are therefore a weaker test.

The predicted cross-trait contrast held (Fig. 4). The inverse-variance-weighted CAGE − DNase contrast was +2.21 for the two traits predicted to be CAGE type and −1.21 for the trait predicted to be DNase type (difference +3.42 ± 0.67, one-sided *P* = 1.6 × 10^−7^). BMD was DNase type, as predicted: stromal DNase τ\* was +1.92, DNase ranked first in all 20 resamples, and it exceeded CAGE (*P* = 0.004) and H3K27ac (*P* = 0.003). Total cholesterol was CAGE type, as predicted: liver CAGE τ\* was +3.70, ranked first in all 20 resamples, and exceeded DNase (*P* = 7 × 10^−5^) and H3K27ac (*P* = 0.004). Neutrophil count ranked CAGE first in 18 of 20 resamples and CAGE exceeded DNase (*P* = 0.008), but it was not distinguished from H3K27ac (*P* = 0.085). By the pre-specified criterion, which required the predicted layer to exceed both others, this prediction was not confirmed. The predicted lineages were enriched for all three traits (Supplementary Table S1): myeloid for neutrophil count (τ\* = +2.04, *P* = 2 × 10^−11^, first of 12 lineages), liver for total cholesterol (+1.97, *P* = 0.002, first), and stromal for BMD (+1.85, *P* = 2 × 10^−10^, third after adipose and skeletal muscle, the same mesenchymal pattern as height).

## Predicted annotations carry information beyond measured annotations and eQTLs, most of it where activity is predicted

We next asked whether the predicted annotations carry heritability information beyond measured data. The question is whether a predicted annotation keeps a positive τ\* when measured annotations are in the same model, not whether its τ\* is larger than theirs, because τ\* depends on the size and distribution of an annotation. The baseline model already contains a fine-mapped eQTL annotation pooled over 44 GTEx tissues [@hormozdiari2018; @gtex2020], so every τ\* in this study is conditional on it. We also built measured annotations for the same ontology terms from the data sources on which AlphaGenome was trained: FANTOM5 CAGE peaks and transcribed enhancers for CAGE [@forrest2014; @andersson2014], and ENCODE DNase-seq and H3K27ac peaks [@encode2020], with windows matched to the scorer windows of the predictions (Methods). These annotations mark positions where a regulatory element is active in the relevant tissue. They covered 0.5–1.2% (CAGE), 5–23% (DNase) and 12–17% (H3K27ac) of common SNPs, against 5% for the predicted annotations.

In the primary test, specified before the comparison was run, the predicted CAGE annotation of each trait's lineage was fitted together with the measured CAGE, DNase and H3K27ac annotations of the same lineage. It remained significant in 8 of 11 traits (false discovery rate < 0.05; Supplementary Table S4); the exceptions were neuroticism, BMI and height. Only about half of the SNPs in the predicted CAGE annotations fell within any measured active window, so the remaining information is not explained by the position of a measured active element. It could reflect the predicted allelic effect itself or activity that the model predicts outside measured peaks; we tested this below.

After the layer map was known, we repeated the comparison at each trait's own layer, which was DNase for height and CAGE for the other ten traits (post hoc; Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S3). This reused existing models except for one new conditional model for height. Conditional on the three measured layers, the predicted own-layer τ\* remained positive in 10 of 11 traits (one-sided *P* < 0.05, uncorrected) and retained a median of 71% of its unconditioned value (Fig. 5a).

The eQTL annotation of the baseline pools bulk GTEx tissues and contains no microglia. We therefore also conditioned on fine-mapped eQTLs from brain cell types and monocytes (FunGen-xQTL [@fungen2026]; maximum SuSiE posterior probability per SNP over microglia, neurons, other glia, bulk brain and monocytes; Methods). For AD and the five brain traits, the own-layer effect annotation retained 89–101% of its τ\* (false discovery rate < 0.05 in all six), and for AD the myeloid CAGE activity annotation retained 98% (Supplementary Table S19). The cell-type eQTL annotations were themselves informative for the matching traits—neuronal eQTLs for schizophrenia, neuroticism and bipolar disorder, monocyte eQTLs for blood and immune traits, and microglial eQTLs for AD in its larger GWAS—so the predicted annotations and the cell-type eQTLs explain largely different parts of heritability.

A predicted allelic effect also carries positional information, because large effects are predicted mainly where an element is predicted to be active. To ask how much of the information lies in the predicted effect of the base change rather than in the predicted location of active elements, we built predicted-activity annotations from the same tracks and windows (the larger of the predicted signals of the two alleles, ranked within each track; Methods) and conditioned the effect annotations on them in three ways (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Tables S15 and S18). With the top 5% of predicted activity as binary covariates, the own-layer effect annotation remained informative in 5 of 11 traits (false discovery rate < 0.05): educational attainment, schizophrenia and bipolar disorder in brain tissue, LDL cholesterol in liver and height (DNase) in the stromal lineage, whereas for AD, monocyte count, IBD and rheumatoid arthritis the effect τ\* fell to 1–14% of its value and an activity annotation carried the information instead (myeloid CAGE activity for AD, +1.91 ± 0.67). With the activity percentile as a continuous covariate, 6 of 11 remained, but the set differed: AD (80% retained), monocyte count, IBD, bipolar disorder, LDL cholesterol and height, with educational attainment and schizophrenia no longer significant. Which traits retained effect information beyond predicted activity therefore depended on how activity was conditioned on, and we do not interpret the difference between lineages.

Effect annotations are concentrated where activity is predicted: 15–23% of the SNPs in the top decile of CAGE activity were in the top-5% effect annotation, against 0.2–4% in the bottom decile. We therefore built effect annotations stratified on activity, taking the top 5% of effects within each of 100 activity strata so that membership was independent of activity. None of the 11 stratified own-layer annotations was informative (all *q* > 0.05; Supplementary Table S18), and their layer contrasts no longer differed between traits (*Q* = 24.0, *P* = 0.24). Averaged over the whole range of activity, the information in the predicted effect annotations therefore lies in where activity is predicted. Because stratification across all activity levels has little power for effects within active elements, where predicted effects are large, we finally compared, within the top 10% of predicted activity, activity-matched halves with larger and smaller predicted effects (post hoc; Fig. 5c). Larger effects carried more heritability for height (DNase, Δτ\* = +2.28 ± 0.49, *q* = 2 × 10^−5^), bipolar disorder (CAGE, +2.73 ± 1.12, *q* = 0.027) and schizophrenia (CAGE, +1.08 ± 0.41, *q* = 0.022), 3 of 11 traits; the same three held with the larger GWAS, and BMD showed the same DNase difference (+2.37 ± 0.49). For AD no difference was detected in either GWAS (CAGE, −0.19 ± 1.66 and +0.35 ± 1.44), and within the top 20% of activity an effect of the size seen for height was excluded.

The layer map itself was not explained by predicted activity. With binary or continuous activity as covariates, the effect contrasts still differed between traits (*Q* = 53.1 and 60.5, *P* = 7.9 × 10^−5^ and 5.9 × 10^−6^) and the brain traits still leaned more towards CAGE than height (Table 1); within predicted active elements, the effect differences Δτ\* also differed between traits (*Q* = 57.8, *P* = 1.5 × 10^−5^; brain traits versus height +3.01 ± 0.83). Predicted activity alone also differed between traits (*Q* = 41.1, *P* = 0.004), but DNase activity ranked first for most traits, including the brain traits, so the CAGE lean of the brain traits was a property of the predicted allelic effects rather than of predicted activity. The DNase lean of height and BMD held under every activity construction (Supplementary Table S18).

Comparisons of the size of predicted and measured τ\*, and of predicted and eQTL τ\*, depend on annotation size and are shown in Supplementary Fig. S5 and Supplementary Table S3; adding the predicted annotation reduced the measured CAGE τ\* by a median of 29% and the eQTL τ\* by a median of 18%.

# Discussion

By aggregating sequence-model predictions across the genome, we obtained a map of complex-trait heritability with two axes. The first axis, the lineage, largely reproduced known biology: brain traits mapped to brain tissue, immune and blood traits to lymphoid and myeloid cells, LDL cholesterol to liver and height to mesenchymal tissues. The second axis, the relative informativeness of predicted layers within the lineage, differed between traits. This difference was significant after differences in track numbers were reduced and covariance between traits was accounted for; it held in every control analysis (Table 1); and for three traits analysed only after their layer had been predicted, the predicted contrast held and the predicted layer was confirmed in two. Conditioning on predicted activity showed that most of the information in the predicted annotations lies where activity is predicted. The layer map nonetheless held conditional on activity, and within predicted active elements larger predicted effects carried additional heritability for height and two psychiatric traits.

Earlier work reported that traits differ in the chromatin marks or layers that are most enriched for their associations [@trynka2013; @trynka2015; @finucane2015; @dong2022]. Those comparisons largely combined marks measured in different sets of biosamples and were descriptive. Predicted effects on several layers for the same variants and lineages allowed us to fit the layers jointly, reduce differences in their track numbers and test the trait-by-layer interaction formally. The lineage axis, the myeloid localization of AD and the mesenchymal localization of height are not new; tissue-specific scores from earlier sequence models are also enriched for heritability in relevant traits [@ye2026]. What is new, to our knowledge, is a formal test of the layer axis and evidence that a trait's position on it carries over to related traits. Our results also agree with Dey et al. [@dey2020], who found that deep-learning allelic-effect annotations added little beyond the models' predictions for the reference sequence: once effect annotations were matched on predicted activity, they carried no detectable information. The layer axis, however, is not a consequence of this. It held with predicted activity in the model, and it reflects which layer's predicted active elements, and for some traits which effects within them, carry a trait's heritability. For predicted transcription initiation, a layer their analysis did not include, the predicted active elements carried heritability beyond fine-mapped tissue and brain cell-type eQTLs in every brain trait and in AD.

The positions of traits on the layer axis are hypotheses about regulatory routes, not demonstrations of them. A larger conditional τ\* can reflect biology, but it can also reflect differences between assays in prediction accuracy, in the quality of the training data, in signal near promoters and in how scorers aggregate signal. With that caveat, skeletal growth and bone formation depend on mesenchymal differentiation programmes, and height heritability is enriched in accessible chromatin of growth-plate chondrocytes [@guo2017; @richard2025]. In measured data, active histone marks of chondrogenic cells are at least as strongly enriched [@finucane2015], so our result concerns predicted allelic effects on accessibility rather than the location of accessible regions. Consistent with this, among activity-matched SNPs within predicted active stromal elements, those with larger predicted DNase effects carried more heritability for height and BMD (post hoc), and the DNase lean of these two traits held with continuous annotations and under every activity construction. This would fit traits in which risk acts partly through base changes that alter enhancer accessibility. For brain traits and LDL cholesterol, the most informative annotation was predicted transcription initiation, which occurs at promoters and enhancers [@andersson2014; @core2014; @tippens2020]. This held at SNPs more than 1 kb from annotated TSSs and, for bipolar disorder and schizophrenia, within predicted active elements, which is consistent with, but does not demonstrate, a contribution of base changes that alter transcription initiation, including enhancer transcription. Genetic effects on promoter usage are context specific and contribute to complex traits [@alasoo2019; @yuan2024], and these predicted CAGE annotations captured heritability not accounted for by fine-mapped eQTLs from bulk tissues or from brain cell types, including microglia. Transcription initiation is therefore a candidate layer for part of the regulation that eQTL studies miss [@umans2021; @connally2022; @mostafavi2023], although whether that part acts through initiation-site choice or through accessibility cannot be separated with these data. We regard this as an interpretation and have not tested it directly.

For AD, the myeloid localization agrees with earlier epigenomic and expression studies [@gjoneska2015; @huang2017; @tansey2018; @novikova2021; @nott2019; @corces2020] and was robust (Supplementary Table S12), and in a larger, overlapping AD GWAS [@ad2026meta] the myeloid lineage, the CAGE lean and the contrast with height were unchanged (Supplementary Table S16). The layer assignment is less secure than the lineage: the excess of CAGE over DNase depended largely on a few regions, especially the *MS4A* region, and conditional on predicted activity the information lay in myeloid CAGE activity rather than in the effect annotation, with no additional effect information detected within predicted active elements in either GWAS. AD heritability is therefore concentrated where transcription initiation is predicted to be active in myeloid cells, a signal not explained by microglial or monocyte eQTLs, rather than at SNPs predicted to change initiation. Because the Atlas contains no microglia tracks, the myeloid lineage stands in for microglia only through shared myeloid programmes [@novikova2021], and the absence of detected brain-tissue enrichment is not evidence against a brain contribution, given the low observed-scale heritability of AD [@kim2024]. Genome-wide aggregation resolved the myeloid lineage that single AD loci had not [@choi2026], but not the layer.

Our study has limitations. First, enrichment of heritability locates where predicted effects co-occur with heritability; it does not identify causal cell types or mechanisms. Second, we analysed common SNPs in European-ancestry GWAS, several of them older and smaller. Replacing six of them with recent, larger GWAS left the heterogeneity and the contrasts with height unchanged, but these GWAS include the earlier participants and are not independent, and in the largest height GWAS DNase was less distinct from CAGE (one-sided *P* = 0.054). Third, the main analyses used binary annotations of the top 5% of SNPs. Heterogeneity between traits held with continuous annotations, but among the SNPs with the largest predicted effects the CAGE lean of the brain, lipid and lymphoid traits was less consistent (Supplementary Table S17). The separation of predicted effects from predicted activity depended on how activity was conditioned on, and the comparison within active elements was post hoc. Fourth, current sequence models capture promoter determinants of expression better than distal enhancers [@karollus2023]. The layer map held at SNPs more than 1 kb from annotated TSSs, but distal CAGE signal may come from unannotated promoters as well as from enhancer RNAs, which our analysis cannot distinguish. Fifth, layers within a lineage come from partly different biosamples, comparisons between traits used different lineages, and the lineage of each map trait was chosen after the lineage map had been seen; the layer types of the map traits are therefore exploratory. Cell-type-matched annotations and comparisons within shared lineages argue against the first two explanations, but matching was at the level of cell type or region rather than donor or developmental stage, and the top layer itself depended on the lineage. For the three validation traits, the lineage and layer were predicted in advance; the lineage was confirmed in all three and the layer in two, and all three are biologically related to their analogues, so the validation shows that a layer type carries over to a related trait, not that it holds for unrelated traits. Sixth, only height and BMD leaned towards DNase, and no trait leaned towards H3K27ac; without height the heterogeneity was smaller and reflected differences in the degree of CAGE dominance (post hoc). Whether other traits lean towards accessibility remains to be tested.

In summary, sequence-based predictions of allelic effects, aggregated genome-wide, show that complex traits differ not only in the tissue but also in the relative informativeness of predicted regulatory layers for their heritability. The layer profile of a trait carried over to related traits. The predicted annotations captured heritability beyond measured annotations and beyond fine-mapped tissue and cell-type eQTLs; most of this information lay in where activity is predicted, and for height and two psychiatric traits also in the size of predicted effects within active elements. Such a map can guide the choice of follow-up assays for a trait, for example accessibility QTLs for a trait whose heritability leans towards accessibility and CAGE, PRO-cap or reporter assays for one that leans towards transcription initiation, and it can supply layer-specific priors for fine-mapping.

# Methods

## GWAS summary statistics

We analysed 11 traits for map construction and three traits for validation (Supplementary Table S1). AD summary statistics were from stage I of the European Alzheimer & Dementia Biobank meta-analysis (*N* = 487,511, including proxy cases) [@bellenguez2022]; the *APOE* region (chr19:43.9–45.9 Mb, GRCh38) was removed. Monocyte count [@vuckovic2020] and the validation traits, heel BMD [@morris2019], neutrophil count [@vuckovic2020] and serum total cholesterol in UK Biobank [@zoodsma2025], were obtained from the GWAS Catalog harmonized files. Signed *Z* scores were computed from effect sizes and standard errors, and the files were processed with munge_sumstats on HapMap3 SNPs [@hapmap2010] excluding the MHC. For BMD the sample size column of the harmonized file was not usable, and the study sample size (426,824) was used for all SNPs. The other nine traits were used as processed LDSC-format files distributed by the Price laboratory: educational attainment and neuroticism [@okbay2016ea; @okbay2016neuro], schizophrenia [@pgc2014], bipolar disorder [@pgc2011], BMI [@speliotes2010], IBD [@jostins2012], rheumatoid arthritis [@okada2014], height [@langoallen2010] and LDL cholesterol [@teslovich2010].

## AlphaGenome Atlas predictions

Target SNPs were all biallelic SNPs with MAF ≥ 5% in the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European reference panel (GRCh38) whose reference and alternative alleles could be matched to the 1000 Genomes high-coverage GRCh38 release (5,828,323 SNPs). We retrieved AlphaGenome Atlas variant scores [@avsec2026; @cheng2026] through the AlphaGenome API for the DNase, CAGE, histone ChIP–seq (H3K27ac, H3K4me1, H3K4me3) and RNA-seq scorers, and the overall variant-impact score (AVI). For each track we stored the absolute value of the raw (uncalibrated) score of the recommended variant scorer. For DNase, CAGE and histone ChIP–seq, the predicted signal of each allele was summed over a window centred on the variant (501 bp for DNase and CAGE, 2,001 bp for histone marks), and the score was log~2~(ΣALT + 1) − log~2~(ΣREF + 1). CAGE tracks are strand specific, and each strand was treated as a separate track. For RNA-seq, the recommended scorer is a gene-level log fold change, and we took the maximum absolute value over genes. Scores were retrieved with the AlphaGenome Python client (version 0.9.0) between 25 September and 1 October 2026; the calibrated quantile scores of the Atlas were not used. SNPs with MAF < 5% or unresolved alleles were assigned 0 in all annotations.

## Lineage assignment

Tracks were assigned to 12 lineages by ordered regular-expression rules on biosample names and types, using metadata only; the rules were fixed before any heritability analysis. Cell-line tracks were excluded. Biosamples that could not be placed in one lineage were left unassigned; these included mixed blood and bone marrow, germ layers, fetal limbs, peripheral nerve and cerebrospinal fluid. Supplementary Table S2 lists track and biosample counts.

## Annotations

For lineage *g* and layer *m*, the SNP score was the maximum absolute allelic effect across the tracks of *g* and *m*. Scores were converted to percentiles across target SNPs, and the top 5% defined the layer annotation. The lineage annotation (Fig. 2) used the mean of the percentiles for DNase, H3K27ac, H3K4me1 and CAGE, again thresholded at the top 5%. H3K4me3 was left out of this score because its annotations were collinear with those of H3K27ac, and RNA-seq because its scores are gene-level log fold changes rather than window-based allelic effects and are not directly comparable with the other layers; both were analysed as separate layers (Supplementary Fig. S2). An all-lineage annotation built in the same way from all assigned tracks served as the control in the lineage-specific model. LD scores were computed with a Python 3 port of LDSC [@bulik2015; @finucane2015] from the 1000 Genomes European reference (1-cM window) for HapMap3 regression SNPs. On chromosome 22, LD scores computed with this implementation reproduced the published baselineLD v2.2 LD scores exactly.

## Stratified LD score regression

All models contained the 97 annotations of baselineLD v2.2 [@gazal2017] and used --overlap-annot, allele frequencies for MAF ≥ 5%, HapMap3 regression weights excluding the MHC and a 200-block jackknife. τ\* was computed as τ~c~ × sd~c~ × *M* / *h*^2^~g~, where τ~c~ is the per-SNP coefficient of annotation *c*, sd~c~ its standard deviation across common SNPs, *M* the number of common SNPs and *h*^2^~g~ the observed-scale SNP heritability. Unless stated otherwise, *P* values for τ\* are one-sided for τ\* > 0. Differences between two τ\* values in the same model were tested with jackknife standard errors computed from the block delete values.

## Layer map

Each trait was analysed in one lineage, chosen after the lineage map from the lineages enriched for that trait and its expected biology (Supplementary Table S1). For two traits this was not the top-ranked lineage. Bipolar disorder was analysed in brain tissue, although the myeloid lineage ranked first in its small GWAS; brain tissue ranked first in a larger GWAS (Supplementary Table S16). Height was analysed in the stromal lineage, which contains chondrocytes, osteoblasts and mesenchymal stem cells, although adipose and skeletal muscle ranked higher. The same lineages were used in the measured-annotation and eQTL comparisons. In the chosen lineage, CAGE, DNase and H3K27ac annotations were fitted jointly. To reduce track-number effects, up to three units per layer were drawn without replacement in each of 20 resamples (seed 20261009). A unit was a track for DNase and H3K27ac and a CAGE library (hCAGE or LQhCAGE), with its two strand-specific tracks, for CAGE; all units were used when three or fewer were available (for example, the two liver CAGE libraries). Estimates and block delete values were averaged over resamples. Because the regression SNPs and jackknife blocks are identical across resamples of a trait, standard errors were computed from the averaged delete values. For each trait, the contrast vector *d* = (CAGE − DNase, CAGE − H3K27ac) was tested for heterogeneity across the 11 traits with a multivariate Cochran's *Q* for a common mean (20 degrees of freedom; see Tests between traits). Three pairwise contrasts were specified in the protocol: AD versus height and four brain traits (educational attainment, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and neuroticism) versus height for CAGE − DNase, and IBD and rheumatoid arthritis versus AD and monocyte count for CAGE − H3K27ac (two-sided, α = 0.05/3). Group means were inverse-variance weighted. A trait was assigned a layer type when that layer had the largest resample-averaged τ\*, ranked first in at least 15 of 20 resamples and exceeded the second layer with one-sided *P* < 0.05; otherwise it was unresolved. Overlap between layer annotations, correlation of their LD scores after regressing out the baseline, variance inflation factors and the convergence of resample averages were computed from existing outputs as descriptive diagnostics (Supplementary Table S13).

## Prospective validation

The three validation traits, their analogues, the predicted lineages and the rule that the predicted layer equals the analogue's layer type were fixed in a dated protocol before the layer map was computed. The resulting predictions were recorded before the validation traits were analysed. For each validation trait we fitted the 12 single-lineage models and the joint three-layer model with the same 20 resamples. The primary test was the one-sided difference in CAGE − DNase between the traits predicted to be CAGE type and those predicted to be DNase type. Secondary tests were enrichment of the predicted lineage (one-sided *P* < 0.05) and confirmation of the predicted layer, which required that layer to exceed both other layers (one-sided *P* < 0.05 for each).

## Threshold sensitivity

Layer annotations were rebuilt with all tracks of each lineage at the top 1%, 0.5% and 0.1% of SNPs, using the same maximum-effect scores and percentiles as the 5% annotations; rebuilding the 5% annotations with the same code reproduced them exactly. The three-layer joint model was fitted once per trait and threshold (42 models), with single-run jackknife standard errors, and the 5% reference was the all-track model of the main analysis. This analysis was specified after the main results had been seen (Supplementary Table S6); its primary criterion at the 0.1% threshold was heterogeneity *P* < 0.05 and a matching top layer in at least 9 of 11 traits, including DNase for height.

## Continuous and non-overlapping annotations
From the same maximum-effect scores and percentiles as the 5% annotations, we built for each lineage and layer a continuous annotation equal to the percentile (0 for SNPs with MAF < 5% or without predictions) and four binary annotations for non-overlapping bins with the boundaries of the threshold analysis (top 0–0.1%, 0.1–0.5%, 0.5–1% and 1–5%); the sums of the bins reproduced the threshold annotations exactly. The three continuous annotations, or the 12 bin annotations, of each trait's lineage were fitted jointly with baselineLD v2.2, with all tracks, for the 14 original GWAS and the five larger GWAS. The primary criteria were heterogeneity *P* < 0.05 with continuous annotations and, for each trait, a CAGE − DNase contrast with the sign of the 5% all-track model in the continuous model and in all four bins (stable) or not (sensitive). Variance inflation factors of the baseline-adjusted LD scores of these annotations were at most 3.4.

## Cell-type-matched layer map
Within each analysed lineage, we grouped the biosamples of the three layers that shared an ontology term or that denoted the same cell type or anatomical region under different terms (Supplementary Table S8). From each group we took one unit per layer (one CAGE library with both strands, or one DNase or H3K27ac track), choosing by a fixed order of terms. Each layer therefore had the same number of units in a lineage: one in myeloid (CD14^+^ monocytes), four in brain tissue, three in stromal, two in liver and five in lymphoid. Annotations were built as in the main analysis from the maximum effect over these units and the top 5% of SNPs, and the joint three-layer model was fitted once per trait in its own lineage. The primary criterion was an unchanged top layer in at least 7 of the 8 traits with a confirmed type, including DNase for height and CAGE for AD.

## Comparison within shared lineages
For each trait we fitted the all-track joint model in every lineage in which the trait was enriched in the lineage map (one-sided *P* < 0.05). In lineages in which both a DNase-type trait (height, BMD) and a CAGE-type trait (AD, monocyte count, educational attainment, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, BMI, LDL or total cholesterol) were enriched, we computed the difference between the inverse-variance-weighted CAGE − DNase contrasts of the two groups. Differences were pooled across lineages by inverse-variance weighting. Because the same traits contributed to several lineages, the criterion used a conservative standard error that assumed perfect correlation between lineages; we also report the standard error from the joint jackknife, which estimates this correlation (Tests between traits). The criterion was a pooled one-sided *P* < 0.05 with the conservative standard error and a positive difference in at least four of six lineages. Both control analyses were specified after the main results had been seen (Supplementary Table S6).

## Proximal and distal annotations
TSSs were the start positions of all GENCODE v46 transcripts of protein-coding and lncRNA genes on chromosomes 1–22 (190,108 unique positions) [@gencode2023]. SNPs within 1,000 bp of a TSS were proximal (5.4% of target SNPs), and the others distal. Each all-track 5% layer annotation of the main map was split into its proximal and distal parts without re-ranking, and the six annotations of a lineage (three layers, each proximal and distal) were fitted jointly with baselineLD v2.2, once per trait in its own lineage, with the joint jackknife across traits. The primary criterion was that, at distal SNPs, CAGE ranked first for at least 5 of the 7 CAGE-type traits and DNase for height, with heterogeneity *P* < 0.05. Because τ\* depends on annotation size, proximal and distal τ\* were not compared with each other.

## Larger GWAS
For height, BMI, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, LDL cholesterol and AD, we repeated the layer, lineage and activity models with recent European-ancestry GWAS: height from the GIANT European meta-analysis including UK Biobank and excluding 23andMe [@yengo2022], BMI from GIANT and UK Biobank [@yengo2018], schizophrenia from the PGC wave 3 European meta-analysis [@trubetskoy2022], bipolar disorder from the PGC European meta-analysis excluding 23andMe [@oconnell2025], LDL cholesterol from the GLGC European meta-analysis including UK Biobank [@graham2021] and AD from the 2026 consensus meta-analysis of 52 European-ancestry studies, in the release without proxy cases (75,638 cases and 1,043,805 controls; GCST90704647) [@ad2026meta]. Summary statistics were processed as for the validation traits, with per-SNP sample sizes (effective sample sizes for the case–control traits), for LDL cholesterol statistics without genomic control, and for AD with the *APOE* region removed. Lineages, annotations, resamples and tests were those of the main analysis; in the updated map, the six traits were replaced and the other five kept. The criteria were heterogeneity *P* < 0.05; for each contrast with height, the same sign as in the main map with two-sided *P* < 0.017; and, for height, DNase ranked first with a negative CAGE − DNase contrast (one-sided *P* < 0.05). The AD GWAS was added after the other five had been analysed, with the same criteria and, in addition, the activity analyses described below.

## Tests between traits
Tests that combined or compared traits used a joint block jackknife. LDSC divides the regression SNPs into 200 blocks of equal size, so block boundaries differ between traits with different SNP sets. We therefore fixed 200 block boundaries by genomic position, dividing the 1,186,879 HapMap3 regression SNPs of the reference weights into blocks of equal size, and refitted every model with these blocks, so that the same genomic segments were deleted for all traits. Point estimates were unchanged. For heterogeneity, the contrasts *d* of all traits were stacked into one vector with its joint jackknife covariance, and *Q* was the generalized least-squares statistic for a common mean (2(*K* − 1) degrees of freedom for *K* traits). We also report an *F* approximation that allows for estimating this covariance from 200 blocks, *F* = (ν − *p* + 1)*Q*/(ν*p*) with *p* = 2(*K* − 1) and ν = 199. Contrasts between groups of traits kept the inverse-variance weights of the main analysis, and their standard errors were computed from the jackknife delete values of the weighted combination, which include the covariance within and between groups. This analysis was specified after the main results had been seen; its values replace those computed with traits treated as independent, which are listed in Supplementary Table S10.

## Influential genomic regions
For the all-track joint models with aligned blocks, each test was recomputed with each block left out, using the stored jackknife delete values, and blocks were ranked by their influence on the test (the full estimate minus the estimate without the block; for *Q*, its decrease). For each test, the regression SNPs of the 5 (primary) or 10 most influential blocks were removed and the traits refitted with unchanged LD scores and a jackknife over the remaining blocks. The criterion was a contrast of the same sign with two-sided *P* < 0.05 (not dependent on a few blocks), the same sign with *P* ≥ 0.05 (weakened) or a change of sign (dependent), and *P* < 0.05 for *Q*.

## Measured annotations

For each lineage, measured annotations were built for the same ontology terms as the Atlas tracks. For CAGE, we used reprocessed FANTOM5 robust CAGE peaks (phase 1 and 2, GRCh38) and transcribed enhancers [@forrest2014; @andersson2014]. FANTOM5 libraries were assigned to a term through the FANTOM5 sample ontology and restricted to libraries of the same sample category (tissue or primary cell) as the Atlas biosample. A peak or enhancer was active for a term when it was expressed (TPM ≥ 1) or used in the majority of that term's libraries. For DNase and H3K27ac we used ENCODE GRCh38 default peak files [@encode2020] (867 files from 683 experiments), and a position was active for a term when it was covered in the majority of the term's experiments. Active positions were extended by ±250 bp (CAGE, DNase) or ±1,000 bp (H3K27ac) to match the scorer windows, and they were combined over the terms of a lineage. Measured annotations covered 0.5–1.2% (CAGE), 5–23% (DNase) and 12–17% (H3K27ac) of common SNPs. We fitted a same-layer model (baselineLD + measured + predicted annotation of one layer) and a full conditional model (baselineLD + the three measured layers + the predicted annotation). In the primary analysis, specified before the comparison was run, the predicted annotation was CAGE for all traits, with false discovery rate control across the 11 traits.

## Comparison with eQTLs

The fine-mapped eQTL annotation of baselineLD (GTEx_eQTL_MaxCPP) [@hormozdiari2018] was compared with the predicted annotation in the same model through the jackknife difference of their τ\* values. Attenuation was the relative change in the eQTL τ\* between the baseline-only model and the model with the predicted annotation.

## Cell-type eQTL annotations

The fine-mapped eQTL annotation of the baseline pools 44 GTEx bulk tissues. To condition also on cell-type eQTLs, we used the single-context SuSiE fine-mapping results of the FunGen-xQTL atlas (NIAGADS NG00184.v1, GRCh38) [@fungen2026] and built five continuous annotations as the maximum posterior inclusion probability per SNP over all genes and contexts of a group (MaxCPP, as in [@hormozdiari2018]; 0 for SNPs not reported): microglia (purified microglia from four brain regions and single-nucleus microglia from three ROSMAP panels), neurons (single-nucleus excitatory and inhibitory neurons), other glia (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and their precursors), bulk brain (eight datasets from three cohorts) and monocytes. Between 83.8% and 97.4% of fine-mapped variants matched target SNPs in each context, and the annotations correlated weakly with the GTEx MaxCPP annotation (*r* = 0.03–0.10). We added the five annotations to the effect-only and the effect + activity models of each trait's lineage. The primary tests, fixed before the analysis, were τ\* > 0 of the own-layer effect annotation for AD and the five brain traits (one-sided, Benjamini–Hochberg across the six) and of the myeloid CAGE activity annotation for AD, with retention relative to the models without cell-type eQTLs; the τ\* of the cell-type eQTL annotations themselves are reported descriptively.

## Predicted-activity annotations

To separate the information in predicted allelic effects from that in predicted activity, we retrieved the Atlas activity scores for all CAGE, DNase and H3K27ac tracks assigned to lineages (784 tracks), with the same windows as the effect scores, and built annotations for the five analysed lineages. The activity score of a track was max(ΣALT, ΣREF), the larger of the predicted signals of the two alleles summed over the window; for most SNPs it is close to the predicted activity of the reference sequence, analogous to the variant-level annotations of Dey et al. [@dey2020]. Because raw activity scores differ in scale between tracks, they were converted to percentiles within each track, and the SNP score of a lineage–layer cell was the maximum percentile over its tracks. Rebuilding the effect annotations with the same code reproduced them exactly. We conditioned on activity in three ways. (i) Binary covariates: the top 5% of the activity score defined an activity annotation, and the three all-track effect annotations of each trait's lineage were fitted jointly with the three activity annotations (effect + activity), with the three activity annotations alone, and with the measured annotations also in the model. The primary test was τ\* > 0 for the own-layer effect annotation in the effect + activity model (one-sided, Benjamini–Hochberg across the 11 map traits), with retention defined as its τ\* as a percentage of that in the effect-only model and the criterion of retention in at least 6 of 11 traits (3–5, partly explained by activity; 2 or fewer, largely explained). (ii) Continuous covariates: the three activity percentiles were fitted as continuous annotations in place of the binary ones, with the same test and criterion. (iii) Stratified effect annotations: target SNPs were divided into 100 strata of equal size by the activity percentile of the same lineage and layer, and within each stratum the top 5% of SNPs by effect were annotated, so that membership was independent of activity; the three stratified annotations were fitted jointly, with the same test and criterion. Analyses (ii) and (iii) were specified after the results of (i) had been seen, with the criterion that the pattern of (i) was robust if reproduced in both. Heterogeneity and the three pairwise contrasts were computed for each model as for the layer map, with the joint jackknife. In a post hoc analysis designed after these results, we restricted to SNPs in the top 10% of predicted activity, divided them into ten strata of one percentile each, and within each stratum split SNPs at the median predicted effect into a larger-effect and a smaller-effect annotation of equal size and activity distribution; the three larger- and three smaller-effect annotations of a lineage were fitted jointly, and the test was Δτ\* = τ\*(larger) − τ\*(smaller) > 0 at the own layer (one-sided, Benjamini–Hochberg across the 11 traits; top 20% of activity as a sensitivity analysis). Variance inflation factors of the baseline-adjusted LD scores were at most 2.6 in (ii), 1.2 in (iii) and 4.7 in the within-active-element models. Activity scores were retrieved with the same client between 17 and 19 October 2026.

## Robustness of the AD lineage enrichment

In the initial analysis, the myeloid annotation was based on monocyte and macrophage tracks, scored with the same percentile mean as the lineage annotation and thresholded at 1%, 5% and 10% or used as a continuous percentile score. We also conditioned on the top 5% of the AVI score and repeated the analysis with summary statistics that retained the *APOE* region. In a post hoc analysis, we removed all SNPs within 1 Mb of genome-wide significant variants (*P* < 5 × 10^−8^) and of the lead variants of 75 reported loci, a total of 162 Mb, and we computed leave-one-block-out estimates over the 200 jackknife blocks (Supplementary Table S12).

## Analysis protocols and levels of evidence

Each analysis stage was specified in a dated protocol before that stage was run, and all S-LDSC models were run sequentially with fixed software and reference files. Because the stages were sequential, later protocols were written with knowledge of earlier results; for example, the layer-map protocol (9 October 2026) was written after the single-layer map and the joint six-layer models (2–4 October), in which a CAGE cell was the strongest for 10 of 11 traits. Supplementary Table S6 lists, for each stage, its protocol date and the results that had been seen when it was written. We therefore distinguish three levels of evidence. The predictions for the three validation traits are prospective: they were fixed before any data for those traits were analysed. The lineage, single-layer and layer maps, the measured-annotation conditioning and the threshold, cell-type-matched, shared-lineage, proximal–distal, joint-jackknife, block-removal, activity (binary, continuous and stratified), cell-type eQTL, larger-GWAS and continuous and bin analyses had hypotheses or criteria fixed before they were run, as given above, but after earlier results had been seen. The leave-one-trait-out heterogeneity, the own-layer comparisons, the size comparisons with measured and eQTL annotations, the comparison within predicted active elements, the removal of loci and leave-one-block-out estimates for AD, and the collinearity, threshold-contrast and resampling diagnostics are post hoc or descriptive; they are labelled in the text and in Supplementary Tables S6 and S12.
